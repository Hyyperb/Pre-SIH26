Advancements in 3D Cadastral Mapping and Urban Land Record Digitization in Pune: Frameworks, Technologies, and Policy Implications
Introduction to the Urban Cadastral Paradigm
The unprecedented pace of urbanization in the metropolitan region of Pune has fundamentally altered the spatial, administrative, and economic requirements of municipal land management. Historically characterized by low-rise traditional architecture in the central Peths, Pune has rapidly expanded into a high-density, vertically stratified metropolis, driven by the proliferation of Information Technology (IT) parks, industrial corridors, and large-scale residential developments. This structural transformation has exposed the inherent limitations of traditional two-dimensional (2D) land administration systems. Historically, the Government of Maharashtra relied heavily on paper-based cadastral maps and textual records to establish the Record of Rights (RoR). Within this framework, the 7/12 extract served as the primary record for rural and agricultural land, while the City Survey (CTS) Property Card—often referred to locally as the Malmatta Patrak—functioned as the definitive document for urban, non-agricultural land1.
While the introduction of the digital Mahabhunaksha platform successfully digitized tens of thousands of village maps and transitioned the state toward a centralized 2D cadastral framework, the multi-layered realities of urban real estate demand a three-dimensional (3D) approach5. The stratification of property ownership in high-rise buildings dictates that multiple, distinct owners occupy the exact same 2D geographical footprint. This structural gap in foundational property documentation has historically complicated property tax assessments, municipal infrastructure planning, structural audits, dispute resolutions, and urban redevelopment processes7.
To bridge this operational and legal chasm, civic and state authorities have initiated a series of advanced 3D mapping and land record modernization programs explicitly tailored for urban environments. These encompass localized municipal initiatives, such as the Pune Municipal Corporation (PMC) 3D Twin Drone Survey, federal scaling models like the National Geospatial Knowledge-based Land Survey of Urban Habitations (NAKSHA) pilot programme, and landmark legislative reforms, most notably the implementation of Vertical Property Cards (VPC) under the amended Maharashtra Land Revenue Code (MLRC), 19668. This report exhaustively analyzes the integration of 3D mapping technologies within the urban areas of Pune, exploring the geospatial methodologies, the legal frameworks governing vertical ownership, the profound economic implications for municipal revenue, and the broader integration with the Mahabhunaksha and Mahabhumi digital ecosystems.
The Cadastral Baseline: Mahabhunaksha and the Urban Property Card
Before examining the complex transition to 3D mapping, it is essential to understand the existing digital baseline managed by the Settlement Commissioner and Director of Land Records, Maharashtra. The Mahabhunaksha portal is the state’s official cadastral mapping software, conceptualized and developed by the National Informatics Centre (NIC) under the federal Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP)13.
The primary utility of the 2D Bhunaksha framework in Pune's urban context has been the establishment of an interactive, web-based interface that allows citizens, financial institutions, and government officials to view georeferenced land maps1. The NIC designed Bhunaksha not merely as a repository for scanned paper maps, but as a dynamic parcel map management system capable of reflecting actual spatial changes arising from property mutations14. Users interact with the platform by selecting their district (Pune), land category (Urban), taluka, village, and specific map type, ultimately querying the database using the unique City Survey (CTS) number assigned to urban plots4.
This 2D system currently facilitates boundary verification, allowing investors and property buyers to examine the exact dimensions, adjoining boundaries, and geometric shape of a land parcel before executing a transaction, thereby mitigating the risk of fraudulent sales and encroachment5. Furthermore, Mahabhunaksha is tightly integrated with the Mahabhulekh geoportal, which hosts the textual Record of Rights13. By cross-referencing the spatial map with the digital Property Card, users can verify the chain of title, existing financial encumbrances, and exact land classification4. The Government of Maharashtra has also elevated the legal status of these digital records. Under Section 5 of the Information Technology Act, 2000, digitally signed Property Cards and 7/12 extracts downloaded from the Mahabhumi portal are granted full legal validity19. These documents bear a unique 16-digit verification number and a QR code, enabling them to be accepted directly by courts, registration offices, and banking institutions for title verification and mortgage origination without requiring physical attestation from the City Survey Office19.
Despite these significant administrative advancements, the 2D Mahabhunaksha system remains fundamentally constrained when applied to Pune's modern urban landscape. The City Survey Property Card records land strictly on a horizontal plane; it identifies the owner of the physical ground plot—frequently the original landowner, a real estate developer, or a cooperative housing society—but fails to document the individual owners of the strata (flats) built vertically above it7. Consequently, individual apartment owners possess a registered sale deed and a society share certificate, but they remain effectively invisible within the state’s foundational land registry11. This critical omission in the 2D framework necessitates the paradigm shift toward 3D cadastral mapping and vertical property registration.
The Impetus for 3D Urban Mapping: Municipal Revenue and the PMC Drone Survey
The operationalization of 3D mapping in Pune is not merely a theoretical exercise in geographic information systems; it is currently being spearheaded by the Pune Municipal Corporation (PMC) to address urgent administrative and financial imperatives. Rapid urbanization and the proliferation of high-density residential and commercial structures have far outpaced the civic body's conventional property assessment mechanisms8.
Under conventional municipal procedures, developers or individual property owners are expected to proactively apply for a property tax assessment after obtaining a completion certificate from the PMC. This application is subsequently followed by a manual physical measurement of the property by civic officials before a tax levy is applied. This archaic process is highly inefficient, lacks systemic transparency, and relies heavily on voluntary compliance, resulting in severe revenue leakage. Administrative estimates indicate that over 400,000 properties in Pune remain entirely outside the official property tax net, representing a massive hemorrhage of civic revenue that could potentially exceed ₹1,000 crore annually if properly assessed8. Furthermore, numerous properties undergo unrecorded alterations in usage—such as illicit conversions from residential to commercial spaces—or execute unauthorized structural additions that traditional 2D maps and manual surveys consistently fail to capture.
To rectify this massive administrative blind spot, the PMC initiated a comprehensive "3D Twin Drone Survey" executed by MahaPREIT (Mahatma Phule Renewable Energy and Infrastructure Technology Limited) operating under the city's Disaster Management Department. The digital survey meticulously compared existing municipal tax records with actual on-ground construction data, enabling authorities to instantly identify properties escaping taxation22.
The technical execution of the project involved highly advanced geospatial data collection techniques designed specifically for complex urban morphology. Aerial drone photogrammetry was utilized to capture high-resolution, multi-angle imagery of the urban landscape. This technique is critical for generating 3D point clouds and extracting accurate building heights across expansive areas8. However, aerial drones are often limited by building overhangs, dense tree canopies, and narrow alleyways typical of older urban centers. To counteract this, vehicle-mounted mobile scanning systems were deployed in tandem to capture street-level detail and structural facades, creating a holistic digital twin of the targeted wards. The resulting 3D maps were deeply integrated with Geographic Information System (GIS) databases, embedding crucial textual data into the 3D physical models. This tagged data included the year building permission was granted, the sanctioned usage type, the approved built-up area, and the construction classification24. By leveraging change detection algorithms against historical satellite imagery and approved architectural plans, the system could automatically flag unauthorized constructions, vertical additions, and spatial encroachments for immediate civic enforcement.
The initial mapping phases achieved significant coverage, successfully charting 260 square kilometers out of Pune’s total 527 square kilometers. This initial phase strategically targeted high-growth, high-value corridors such as Baner, Balewadi, Aundh, and Shivajinagar. The financial efficacy of the 3D mapping approach was conclusively proven during a localized pilot analysis of the captured data. MahaPREIT identified a small cluster of highly anomalous properties that had entirely escaped standard assessment.

In addition to the 78 completely unregistered properties detailed above, another 12 properties were identified with grossly incorrect tax assessments. Consolidating these figures, the total projected civic recovery for just 90 targeted properties amounted to approximately ₹75 crore.
However, the transition to an automated, drone-driven 3D cadastre is fraught with administrative and governance challenges. Despite the technological success of the survey, its widespread institutionalization within the PMC encountered severe administrative friction due to revenue-sharing disputes. MahaPREIT demanded a highly lucrative 30 percent share of the recovered property tax amount as compensation for conducting the survey, processing the spatial data, and executing the identification processes. The PMC’s Property Tax Department categorically rejected this demand, deeming it financially unviable and structurally detrimental to municipal fiscal sovereignty. Funneling nearly one-third of foundational municipal tax recoveries to a surveying agency sets a precarious precedent for the financialization and privatization of urban data. Consequently, the PMC halted the acceptance of the recovery files and explored alternatives, such as directly contracting the private drone mapping company originally utilized by MahaPREIT, offering a significantly lower compensation rate of approximately 9 percent.
This dynamic is not unique to the PMC. In the neighboring Pimpri-Chinchwad Municipal Corporation (PCMC), a similar drone-based property survey executed by Sthapatya Consultant India Private Limited came under intensive scrutiny25. The private agency surveyed three lakh more properties than the designated target, resulting in an additional payout of ₹29 crore, prompting an inquiry by the State Industry Minister into allegations of bogus measurements, incorrect entries, and unauthorized tax levies. These incidents underscore a vital second-order insight: while 3D GIS technologies provide unparalleled capabilities for municipal revenue generation and cadastral accuracy, the procurement models, data ownership protocols, and inter-agency financial agreements must be strictly regulated to prevent administrative deadlocks and systemic exploitation.
Technological Framework of 3D Cadastral Mapping: NAKSHA and Survey of India
While the PMC's drone surveys represent aggressive, localized municipal initiatives driven by immediate revenue needs, the underlying methodology for scaling urban 3D mapping is being rigorously standardized at the federal level through the NAKSHA (National Geospatial Knowledge-based Land Survey of Urban Habitations) programme10. Launched by the Department of Land Resources (DoLR) under the Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP), and utilizing the Survey of India (SoI) as its core technical partner, NAKSHA is a pioneering initiative aiming to revolutionize urban land records across 157 Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) in India10. Backed by a substantial financial outlay of ₹193.81 crore, the programme focuses on advanced aerial surveys, rigorous feature extraction, and the seamless integration of existing legacy records26.
The urban morphology of a metropolis like Pune—which encompasses the meticulously planned, grid-like expansions of the IT corridors, alongside the highly congested, organic vertical growth of the ancient central Peths—requires a flexible, multi-sensor approach to surveying10. The NAKSHA Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) deploys three distinct methodologies explicitly tailored to navigate diverse urban complexities:
2D Nadir Camera Surveying: Utilized primarily for planned, horizontal urban sections with flat roofs, minimal vertical stacking, and clear roadways. The nadir (straight-down) cameras capture precise vertical imagery to generate an Orthorectified Image (ORI), a basic Digital Elevation Model (DEM), and standard 2D datasets26. This fundamental approach mirrors the technologies utilized in the rural SVAMITVA scheme, prioritizing boundary identification over volumetric analysis10.
Oblique Angle Camera Surveying (3D): For urban zones characterized by significant vertical expansion, such as Pune's dense apartment complexes and commercial high-rises, standard Nadir imagery is wholly insufficient as it fails to capture architectural facades. To overcome this, NAKSHA employs advanced five-camera oblique systems that capture highly angled imagery. This multidimensional data facilitates the generation of intricate 3D reality models, comprehensive 3D GIS datasets, and accurate measurements of vertical structural boundaries, enabling true spatial volume analysis26.
Oblique Camera Integrated with LiDAR Sensors: In the most challenging urban topographies—highly congested old city areas with narrow clearances, or regions obscured by dense tree canopy—photogrammetry alone struggles to accurately determine the bare earth surface necessary for foundational mapping. The integration of Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors allows active laser pulses to penetrate canopy gaps and reflect directly off the true ground surface10. This combined hardware approach yields highly precise bare-earth DEMs alongside high-fidelity 3D building models, providing an exact topographic baseline.
The implementation of these sophisticated technologies requires a massive logistical and capacity-building effort. Under Phase 3 of the NAKSHA programme, the government has prioritized the training of 1,826 field survey teams across participating states31. These teams, coordinated through State Administrative Training Institutes (ATIs), are equipped with Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) Rovers, Electronic Total Stations (ETS), and specialized Field Data Collectors (FDCs) to ground-truth the aerial data and execute precise land parcel demarcation26. The ultimate goal is to process the acquired data to generate Ortho Rectified Imagery, extract features based on visual interpretation, and hand over the validated models to respective state departments on an end-to-end WebGIS platform26.
Data Modeling, AI Integration, and Interoperability Standards
To ensure that the 3D data generated by these diverse surveys remains interoperable across various municipal, state, and federal departments—such as Town Planning, the Inspector General of Registration (IGR), and Disaster Management—it must adhere to strict, internationally recognized geospatial standards33. The CityGML (Geography Markup Language for 3D City Models) standard serves as the foundational schema for organizing this vast influx of urban geospatial data29.
CityGML fundamentally categorizes 3D models into varying Levels of Detail (LOD), which dictates both the computational weight of the model and the semantic richness of the underlying data29. This stratification is critical for tailoring the 3D map to specific municipal use cases without overwhelming civic IT infrastructure.

Processing this multi-layered data requires immense computational capacity and advanced algorithmic intervention. The modern NAKSHA SOP and associated academic frameworks outline the heavy reliance on automated feature extraction algorithms. Recent studies demonstrate that machine learning models—specifically Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT)—can be trained on high-resolution aerial imagery and OpenStreetMap Point of Interest (POI) data to automatically classify building usage (e.g., distinguishing between residential, commercial, and industrial facilities)23. Furthermore, these models utilize morphological filtration techniques to estimate building heights in data-limited settings.
By integrating machine learning algorithms with the 3D volumetric data of buildings extracted from CityGML LOD1 or LOD2 models, demographic researchers and urban planners can generate extremely high-resolution micro-population maps. By estimating human occupancy based on structural volume and classified spatial usage, planners can accurately project localized utility demand—such as water consumption, electricity load, and solid waste generation—at a highly granular, ward-by-ward level, far exceeding the capabilities of traditional choropleth census maps.
Furthermore, the geospatial community is increasingly leveraging open-source pipelines to democratize access to these complex 3D models. Frameworks utilizing free software, such as Blender for geospatial data processing and the Godot Engine for real-time rendering, allow for the transformation of heavy CityGML data into lightweight, interactive 3D applications29. This enables participatory planning workshops and allows local ward officials, who may lack access to high-end enterprise GIS software, to navigate and interact with the 3D urban environment using standard desktop hardware29.
Legalizing the Vertical Dimension: The Vertical Property Card (VPC)
The technological generation of exquisite 3D urban models via drones and LiDAR provides unparalleled physical data, but physical mapping remains administratively inert without a corresponding legal framework. The most transformative policy development in Maharashtra’s land administration history is the impending introduction of the Vertical Property Card (VPC), a direct legal corollary to 3D cadastral mapping7.
To grant statutory backing to vertical land rights, the state government initiated critical amendments to the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code (MLRC), 1966. Specifically, this legal reform impacts the Record of Rights governed under Section 148 of the MLRC, which traditionally mandates the recording of all holders, occupants, and mortgagees of land on a horizontal basis12.
The VPC represents a monumental paradigm shift from a presumptive land title system—historically based on an accumulation of individual sale deeds—to an explicit, government-certified registry of strata title13. Prior to the VPC, an apartment owner in Pune possessed a registered sale deed and a cooperative housing society share certificate, but the ultimate land record (the City Survey Property Card) only reflected the name of the collective society or the original land developer11. The VPC rectifies this systemic invisibility by stitching the individual strata ownership directly into the state’s primary land registry13.
The state government, guided by an eight-member committee headed by the Additional Chief Secretary (Revenue), has structured the VPC to act as an independent, supplementary record of rights for each individual flat40. It is explicitly designed not to replace the parent City Survey Property Card or the registered sale deed, but to exist alongside them, forming a comprehensive matrix of ownership7.
A highly detailed VPC will contain several critical data points authenticated by the state:
Identity Metrics: The full legal names of the flat owner(s) alongside a unique digital verification code (such as a QR code) linking securely back to the Mahabhumi servers7.
Spatial Coordinates: The specific building wing, floor number, designated flat number, and the legally recognized carpet area measured in square feet or square meters.
Undivided Share of Land (UDS): The defining mathematical feature of the VPC is the explicit documentation of the owner's proportional share in the underlying plot. This is typically calculated as a ratio of the individual flat's carpet area against the total carpet area of all units within the building7. While the physical land remains undivided and common to all society members, the fractional legal interest is now officially quantified and enforceable7.
Encumbrances and Financial Liabilities: Crucially for the financial sector, the VPC will dynamically record any bank loans, mortgages, or legal liens secured specifically against the individual apartment unit22.
The issuance of the VPC is designed as a staggered, phased rollout. Beginning in January 2026, all newly constructed projects registered with the Maharashtra Real Estate Regulatory Authority (MahaRERA) are slated to be integrated automatically; a flat's registration simply cannot be completed without the simultaneous issuance of a VPC7. For existing buildings and cooperative housing societies in Pune, the process requires proactive engagement. Individual applications are prohibited to ensure data integrity; instead, a cooperative society must pass a formal resolution at its Annual General Meeting (AGM) and apply collectively7. The government has outlined a nominal one-time fee of ₹500 per flat, with a targeted compliance deadline extending to December 2027 for older structures7.
The convergence of 3D physical mapping and the Vertical Property Card yields profound third-order effects on Pune's real estate economy. Firstly, it promises frictionless urban redevelopment. Many older buildings in central Pune and early IT-boom structures in areas like Kothrud and Aundh are approaching the end of their structural lifecycles. Redevelopment negotiations frequently stall over bitter disputes regarding individual land share allocations and precise carpet area measurements39. By providing a mathematically exact, state-certified record of the UDS, the VPC eliminates the ambiguities that traditionally trigger prolonged litigation during redevelopment22.
Secondly, the VPC injects vital liquidity and security into the banking sector. Financial institutions face significant risks when underwriting mortgages based purely on builder-issued documents or housing society NOCs, leading to systemic issues like double-mortgaging (fraudulently securing loans on the same flat from multiple banks)11. The VPC acts as a centralized, tamper-proof collateral registry. Financial institutions can instantly verify clear title, exact carpet area, and existing liens via the QR code, dramatically expediting home loan approvals and significantly reducing systemic lending risks7.
Finally, the VPC harmonizes seamlessly with existing cooperative laws. It layers onto the legal framework of the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies (MCS) Act, 1960, converting the "implied" equal share of a society member into an "explicit" individual right. Importantly, it does this without disrupting the collective governance mechanisms, redevelopment voting thresholds, or majority requirements mandated for day-to-day society operations11.
Inter-departmental Integration: Creating a Unified Urban Geospatial Ecosystem
The ultimate value of modernizing urban land records in Pune lies in systemic interoperability. The Office of the Settlement Commissioner and Director of Land Records (Maharashtra State) is actively orchestrating an Integrated Land Management System (ILMS) designed to permanently break down data silos across government agencies33.
Currently, the state manages multiple distinct digital platforms: Mahabhunaksha for 2D spatial mapping, Mahabhulekh for textual records (7/12, 8A, Property Cards), eMojani 2.0 for physical land measurement, and the IGRS (Inspector General of Registration and Stamps) portal for property registration and the generation of Index II documents3.
The integration roadmap for Pune's 3D mapping initiative involves feeding the CityGML LOD 2 and LOD 3 datasets generated by drone surveys—such as those overseen by MahaPREIT or the Survey of India under NAKSHA—directly into the Mahabhunaksha geoportal6. Simultaneously, the newly minted Vertical Property Cards will dynamically link to the IGRS database. When a property transaction occurs and a new Index II is generated at the sub-registrar's office, the VPC and the corresponding 3D cadastral database are automatically updated in real-time, executing a seamless digital mutation (e-Ferfar)19. This fully automated loop ensures that the digital twin of the city remains perpetually current, eliminating the historical backlog of mutation entries that have plagued the revenue department for decades.
Beyond the administration of individual property rights, the 3D GIS ecosystem radically transforms macro-level city planning and regulatory enforcement. By combining 3D building models (capturing exact volumes and footprints) with topographical DEMs, the Pune Municipal Corporation can execute highly sophisticated urban simulations.
A critical application is hydrological and flood modeling. Given Pune's inherent vulnerability to seasonal flooding along the Mula-Mutha riverbed, highly accurate bare-earth models derived from LiDAR allow urban planners to map exact blue and red flood lines with unprecedented precision24. The integration of 3D building data permits the calculation of precise volumetric water displacement, enabling the design of resilient flood defenses and the formulation of highly targeted evacuation protocols during extreme weather events.
Furthermore, the Unified Development Control and Promotion Regulations (UDCPR) 2020 for Maharashtra mandate stringent requirements regarding building permissions, Floor Space Index (FSI) calculations, and distances from environmental and infrastructural boundaries (e.g., railways, highways, and high-tension electric lines)45. A comprehensive 3D cadastre allows the Town Planning department to algorithmically verify whether proposed architectural plans comply with UDCPR volumetric and spatial constraints before any physical construction begins, vastly reducing the incidence of unauthorized deviations45.
Finally, massive infrastructure corridor planning—such as the ongoing Pune Metro expansions (Swargate-Katraj underground sections) and the proposed High Capacity Mass Transit Route (HCMTR)—requires flawlessly accurate spatial data to manage complex land acquisition, assess structural impact on surrounding high-rises, and plan the diversion of underground utilities24. The 3D cadastral map provides the foundational geometry required to engineer these civil megaprojects without destabilizing the existing urban fabric.
Current Challenges and the Road Ahead
While the conceptual transition to 3D urban mapping in Pune is remarkably robust, several critical challenges require ongoing strategic management by state authorities.
The primary obstacle is legacy data accuracy and georeferencing constraints. Translating century-old, paper-based City Survey sheets and historical development plans into accurate digital formats frequently reveals deep inconsistencies. Ground-truthing these old records against high-precision, drone-derived 3D data inevitably exposes decades of unauthorized land usage, unrecorded subdivisions, and historical encroachments33. Harmonizing these physical discrepancies with the legal record without triggering mass civic unrest requires exceptionally careful policy formulation, potentially relying on compounding fees, targeted regularisation schemes, or amnesties rather than strict, widespread demolition.
Secondly, hardware and computational limitations pose a significant bottleneck. Managing, hosting, and querying a city-wide 3D dataset formatted in data-heavy schemas like CityGML requires substantial, highly resilient cloud infrastructure and advanced database engineering. The state must ensure that platforms like Mahabhumi and Mahabhunaksha possess the server elasticity to handle high-bandwidth 3D models alongside the millions of daily requests for standard 2D property cards and 7/12 extracts29.
Lastly, regulatory adoption and stakeholder resistance remain potent threats. As evidenced by the PMC-MahaPREIT compensation dispute and the PCMC survey irregularities, inter-agency friction and misaligned financial incentives can easily derail technological deployment8. The state government must establish firm, transparent financial guidelines regarding data acquisition costs, procurement contracts, and revenue sharing, ensuring that municipal bodies retain fiscal sovereignty over the tax revenues generated from these mapping insights.
Conclusion
The evolution of land records in the urban sectors of Pune represents a fundamental paradigm shift from historical, two-dimensional paper archiving to a dynamic, three-dimensional geospatial ecosystem. By moving decisively beyond the limitations of the conventional Mahabhunaksha portal, the integration of 3D Twin Drone Surveys, multi-sensor LiDAR technologies, and the comprehensive NAKSHA programme is equipping the Pune Municipal Corporation with unprecedented, granular visibility into its urban morphology.
Crucially, this massive technological leap is matched by progressive, foundational legislative reform. The amendment of the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, 1966, to institute Vertical Property Cards ensures that the physical realities of high-rise living are finally recognized within the state's legal framework. For individual citizens, this translates to ironclad title security, smoother access to financial credit, and streamlined, transparent redevelopment processes. For the civic administration, it promises the closing of massive property tax loopholes, enhanced disaster management capabilities, and the robust foundation for a true Smart City digital twin.
Moving forward, the successful realization of this 3D cadastral vision in Pune will depend heavily on maintaining stringent data standards (such as CityGML), resolving inter-agency administrative and financial friction, and ensuring continuous, real-time integration between the state's registration, revenue, and municipal databases. As these legal and technological frameworks mature, Pune stands uniquely positioned to serve as the definitive, scalable blueprint for 3D urban land administration across India.
